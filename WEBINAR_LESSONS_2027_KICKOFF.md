## 0. The one-line framing

**"Same knowledge. Which edge cuts depends on the hands."** A case study is a sword: well built, it protects and empowers a community; built carelessly, it steers decisions the wrong way. Your three choices decide the outcome: **the question, the evidence, and the side effects.**

- Protect and empower → evidence you can trace and check; a clear question you can answer; side effects counted before you act.
- Harm when careless → numbers with no source or year; a question too big to answer; pollution simply moved downstream.

## 1. How research actually works

### 1.1 Three phases (use these as your work plan)
| Phase | What it means | Questions to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Conceptualize** | Decide what to study | What motivates the work? What is new about it? What does it add to published work? |
| **2. Execute** | Do the work | Do we have the people, time, and money to finish? What can we do better than others? |
| **3. Document** | Write it up | Can we describe the whole process clearly, briefly, and convincingly? |

Most student projects fail at phase 1 (vague question, no gap) or phase 3 (cannot explain the method). Plan all three from day one.

### 1.2 The two tests every idea must pass
**Test 1 — Significance: does it matter?**
- Is the problem an important issue today?
- Why do you want to study it?
- How does it fit the bigger picture?
- Who would actually use your results?

**Test 2 — Novelty: is it new?**
- Has similar work been done before?
- How is yours different from earlier work?
- Does a database search confirm the gap?
- If no one has tried it, is it worth solving?

> Lesson: "new" is not "never mentioned." It can be a new site, new data, a new combination, a new comparison of options — but you must be able to say what the gap is in one sentence.

### 1.3 Good ideas start from what others already did
Where gaps come from:
- Recent **review papers** — they summarize a field and name what is still unknown.
- **"Future work" sections** — authors list what they could not finish.
- **Conferences and conversations** — talks with teachers and researchers spark questions.
- **Diverse teams** — people of different ages, genders, and cultures see different problems.

The habit behind it: **read the literature.** Know the history of the problem, know what has already been done, and ask how it can be improved.

### 1.4 Pick methods that can actually answer your question
1. **Match** — the method answers *your* question or hypothesis.
2. **Design** — plan tests whose results actually speak to the hypothesis.
3. **Be realistic** — do only what your tools, time, and skills allow.
4. **Get enough** — gather enough data to support a sound conclusion.
5. **Be fair** — analyze the data honestly, even when it surprises you.

**Short on resources?** Focus on what you can do well: careful reasoning, computer models, small focused experiments. (A 12-month, Php 10M pilot with a student team is exactly a "small focused experiment" situation.)

### 1.5 Why publishing your thinking pays off
Reynolds (1895) published on when smooth flow turns turbulent. That single paper fed **aircraft and car design, weather forecasting, and blood flow in arteries.** Foundational, well-documented work travels far beyond its original question.

Also asked in the session: which of these began as a research paper — touchscreens, Google search, Wi-Fi and mobile phones? The point: **all of them.** Research is not an academic ritual; it is where technology comes from.

## 2. The thinking toolkit (PSE / sustainability modeling)

### 2.1 Why systems thinking
**"Sustainability problems never come alone."** Population growth and climate change sit at the center of: water scarcity, energy demand, food supply, disease outbreaks, more severe weather, waste and pollution.

> **Fix one part alone and the problem can move elsewhere.** Models of the whole system make the trade-offs visible.

This is the intellectual core of the whole competition: an estero cleanup that only relocates trash or only hides sewage is not a solution.

### 2.2 How models turn hard questions into clear choices
1. **Map the system** — track every flow of water, energy, and materials.
2. **Write the math** — the balances and limits the system must obey.
3. **Set the goal** — lowest cost, least water, or least carbon.
4. **Get the answer** — the computer tests the combinations.

Worked example (sharing water in an industrial park):
- **Question:** Which plants can reuse each other's wastewater?
- **Model:** water balances for every plant, purity limits for every reuse.
- **Answer:** the pipe network that uses the least fresh water while every plant still gets usable water.

### 2.3 Three ideas that shape sustainability modeling
| Idea | Plain meaning | Why it matters for your case |
|---|---|---|
| **Industrial ecology** (Frosch & Gallopoulos, 1989) | Factories as an ecosystem: one plant's waste becomes another plant's raw material | Your "waste" may be someone's input — look for the exchange, not just the disposal |
| **Life cycle assessment** | A nutrition label for products: tallies water, energy, emissions from raw material to disposal | Shows hot spots and reveals the better option; prevents shifting burden between stages |
| **Input–output analysis** (Leontief, Nobel Prize 1973) | How sectors depend on each other; traces how a change in one sector ripples through the rest | Explains second-order effects on the local economy |

### 2.4 Input–output in action: when a typhoon hits
System boundary: agriculture ↔ energy ↔ manufacturing, with water/land/fuel in and wastes/emissions out.
1. Farms lose crops.
2. Food makers run short of inputs.
3. Shops and transport lose sales.
4. **The model ranks which sectors to help first.**

> Lesson for the case: a waterway intervention has knock-on effects on livelihoods, supply chains, and LGU budgets. Show the ripple, not just the trash count.

### 2.5 Where these models get applied (the speaker's own work)
- **Saving water and materials** — water exchange networks for factories in an eco-industrial park, and how incentives steer them (Avise et al., 2009; 2010).
- **Cleaner energy systems** — polygeneration plant designs that stay worthwhile when prices swing (Sy et al., 2016).
- **Sustainable supply chains** — the mix of bioenergy technologies that meets land, water, and carbon targets (Tan et al., 2009).
- **Disasters and risk** — how damage from a disaster ripples through energy supply chains and the economy (Avise et al., 2015).

## 3. This year's challenge (the actual brief)

**"Pilot a cleaner waterway for Php 10 million."**

| Constraint | Detail |
|---|---|
| **1 kilometer** | One stretch of a creek, tributary, or estero, anywhere in the country |
| **12 months** | A proof-of-concept pilot — **not** a nationwide overhaul |
| **Php 10 million** | **At least 70% on one focus stream**, the rest on one supporting activity |

**From pilot to river system** (the scaling story judges want):
your pilot (12 months, Php 10M) → repeat in similar esteros → same approach, more barangays → explain how it scales → river system (Pasig–Tullahan–Meycauayan).

> Read this as: **depth over breadth.** One credible 1-km result beats a grand plan you cannot deliver or measure.

## 4. Building the case, step by step

### Step 1 — Characterize your target site (answer these five, in writing)
- Where is it, and how long is your stretch?
- What is the main source of pollution?
- How bad is it today? (**your baseline**)
- Who lives and works along it?
- Which rules and programs already apply?

### Step 2 — Build your evidence base (three tiers, all cited)
| Tier | Examples | What it gives you |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Your own surveys, interviews, observations, measurements — e.g., weekly trash count at your site | Baseline and local truth |
| **Published** | Peer-reviewed papers and technical reports from universities and agencies — e.g., a study on Manila trash traps | Method and comparison |
| **Official** | Laws and agency data: RA 9275, NSSMP, DENR-EMB, PSA — **with the year named** — e.g., PSA 2024 wastewater data | Authority and legal footing |

**Check every source:** *Who made it? When was it made? How do they know?*
Trust scale: viral post / blog with no author ← → peer-reviewed paper / agency data with year.

> Rule: every number in your paper carries a source **and** a year. "Numbers with no source or year" is on the slide as a symptom of careless work.

### Step 3 — See the whole system
Put "the waterway" at the center and answer each actor's question:
- **Households and fisherfolk** — who is affected?
- **Businesses and factories** — who pays or profits?
- **LGUs and agencies** — who decides?
- **Estero and Manila Bay** — what changes downstream?

### Step 4 — Decide where to start: environment first, then law, then cost
**1. Environmental** — What is wrong, how bad, and where does it come from?
- Walk the site and take photos.
- Count or weigh trash; check water data.
- **This becomes your baseline.**

**2. Legal** — Who is responsible, and what rules already apply?
- RA 9275 — Clean Water Act
- RA 9003 — Solid Waste Act
- PD 1067 — Water Code (easements)
- Barangay and city ordinances

**3. Financial** — What does it cost, and who pays?
- Prices from suppliers or similar projects.
- Who pays: LGU, businesses, or partners.

> **If you are stuck, ask one question first: what is the main source of pollution at your site? Everything else follows.** (This is the single most useful sentence in the whole session.)

## 5. Pitfalls — the "spot the flaw" drill

Bad proposal: *"We will clean the entire Pasig River with a Php 2 billion tunnel."*

| # | Flaw | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Too big** | "Entire Pasig River" — the challenge asks for a 1-km pilot first |
| 2 | **Over budget** | Php 2 billion is 200× the Php 10 million cap |
| 3 | **Nothing to measure** | No baseline, no target, no 12-month timeline to show it worked |
| 4 | **Treats a symptom** | A tunnel moves dirty water; it does not stop trash or sewage at the source |
| 5 | **No people** | No households, barangay, or businesses named — who acts, and who pays? |
| 6 | **No comparison** | Why a tunnel? No other options were scored or weighed |

The six flaws map exactly to the six steps: **scope → budget → measurement → root cause → people → comparing options.** Use them as a self-audit before submitting.

Also from the interactive exercises:
- *"Does a plastic bag ban reduce trash in the estero?"* → The method that answers it is **count trash at one spot every week, before and after the ban.** Not asking residents' opinions, not reading news, not interviewing the captain. **Measure, before and after.**
- *Which technologies began as research papers?* → **All of them.** Research is the origin of ordinary technology.

## 6. What editors and judges actually look for

| Criterion | Question they are asking |
|---|---|
| **A clear gap** | What is the problem, and why does it matter **now**? |
| **A sound method** | Can a reader follow and **repeat** how you reached the answer? |
| **A real contribution** | What does the reader **know or can do** after reading it? |

> **State limits honestly. Overclaiming is a common reason for rejection.**

## 9. The five sentences to remember

1. Same knowledge — which edge cuts depends on your question, evidence, and side effects.
2. Every idea must pass two tests: does it matter, and is it new?
3. Pick a method that can actually answer your question, and be honest with the data.
