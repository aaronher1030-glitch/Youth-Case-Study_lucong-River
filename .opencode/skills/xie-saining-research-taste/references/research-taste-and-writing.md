# Research Taste And Writing

Use this reference for papers, proposals, talks, project naming, figures, tables, and presentation polish.

## Taste Is Judgment Plus Aesthetic Discipline

Research taste is not only topic selection. It combines:

- philosophical clarity about what matters,
- sensitivity to essential structure rather than surface prestige,
- disciplined attention to details,
- and a personal way of telling the story.

Use taste to decide what to remove as much as what to add.

## Do Not Worship Appearances

Do not overfit to acceptance, hype, famous labels, or fashionable terminology. Ask whether the work reaches the underlying truth or only imitates the surface of successful research.

When reviewing a draft, flag:

- claims that sound important but do not change understanding,
- terms borrowed from trend language without necessity,
- experiments that exist only because reviewers expect them,
- figures that look technical but do not explain a decision.

## Paper As Communication Interface

A paper is a vessel for knowledge. It is written for other people, not for the author's private memory. Treat layout, wording, tables, and figures as part of the content.

Check:

- Does every paragraph have one job?
- Does every figure answer a real question?
- Are tables readable with minimal ink?
- Is terminology stable?
- Are awkward line breaks, lonely words, and visual clutter removed?
- Can a reader reconstruct the decision path?

## Storytelling Like Film

A good research story is not a chronological dump. It should show how the work arrived at its final form.

Build the story around decisions:

1. What did we believe at the start?
2. What failed or surprised us?
3. What did the failure reveal?
4. Which design choice followed from that revelation?
5. Why does the final method now feel inevitable?

The reader should learn how to think, not only what result to cite.

## Naming

Names are part of taste. A good name should carry the core idea, be memorable, and avoid inflated claims.

Evaluate a name by asking:

- Does it reveal the method's main move?
- Is it easy to say and remember?
- Does it avoid pretending to be broader than the evidence?
- Could the name help readers organize the field?
