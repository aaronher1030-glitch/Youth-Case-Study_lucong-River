I. Context
Water plays a central role in sustaining human life, supporting
agriculture and industry, and maintaining healthy aquatic
ecosystems. However, in urban centers across the Philippines,
water bodies have turned into dumping channels for land-
based pollution. Untreated household sewage, uncollected and
unsegregated garbage, and industrial waste continuously flow
into municipal storm drains and open canals (esteros). This
contamination severely degrades freshwater habitats,
intensifies urban flooding, and carries heavy toxic loads directly
into coastal bays and oceans. Economically vulnerable urban
communities, fisherfolk, agricultural workers, and marine
biodiversity bear the heaviest burden of these deteriorating
conditions, facing heightened public health risks and degraded
livelihoods rooted in decades of poor waste management.
To address these environmental threats, the Philippine
government passed Republic Act No. 9275 (The Philippine Clean
Water Act of 2004), establishing a legal framework for water
quality management, wastewater discharge standards, and
pollution prevention [1]. Under this law, the Department of
Public Works and Highways (DPWH) introduced the National
Sewerage and Septage Management Program (NSSMP) to offer
technical and financial support to local government units (LGUs)
and water districts expanding urban sanitation networks [2].
Furthermore, private water concessionaires have deployed

decentralized modular treatment facilities, such as Maynilad’s
modular effluent treatment infrastructure, to clean municipal
wastewater before discharging it into receiving water bodies [3]. 
Despite these laws and capital investments, physical treatment
infrastructure and enforcement mechanisms remain severely
outdated and ineffective relative to the scale of the crisis [4].
These failures stem not only from financial constraints, but also
from weak regulatory enforcement by local government units,
overlapping agency mandates, and a lack of technical expertise
within local water utilities. Consequently, municipal
interventions routinely rely on reactive, temporary measures,
such as environmental river dredging, rather than addressing
pollution at its origin through upstream containment and
proactive governance [5].
Across Philippine cities, water pollution is driven by three
primary compounding factors:
1. Uncollected Municipal Solid Waste: The Philippines produces
approximately 61,000 metric tons of municipal solid waste
every day [6]. In Metro Manila alone, roughly 40% of municipal
waste remains uncollected due to inefficient collection
networks. This unmanaged waste clogs drainage systems,
worsens seasonal urban flooding, and leaks into rivers. The
Asian Development Bank (ADB) estimates that 74% of all plastic

pollution, with about 20% of the 2.7 million annual metric tons
leaking directly into marine ecosystems. Global oceanographic

Philippine rivers are among the world's largest single-country
sources of ocean plastic emissions, with the Pasig River System

2. Inadequate Municipal Sewerage Infrastructure: Centrally

most cities. Instead, most households rely on poorly built,
unmaintained, or unemptied septic tanks, allowing untreated
wastewater (blackwater) to seep directly into groundwater
aquifers and local streams. United Nations Sustainable
Development Goal (SDG) tracking indicates that only 68.0% of
domestic wastewater in the Philippines was safely managed as
of 2024, leaving nearly one-third of all household effluent
completely untreated [8].
3. Unmonitored Commercial and Industrial Run-off: National
environmental monitoring by the DENR Environmental
Management Bureau revealed that 54% of inspected
commercial and industrial facilities (4,930 out of 9,060
establishments nationwide) violated national effluent discharge
standards [9]. High volumes of untreated chemical and organic

II. Scope Definition
Proposals will be evaluated on technical viability, budget
realism, legal alignment, and practical execution. Each team is
provided an initial seed funding cap of Php 10 Million, which is
strictly reserved for a 12-month Pilot Proof-of-Concept. To
ensure spatial feasibility and precise monitoring, the physical
setup of this pilot project must be limited to a single 1-kilometer
stretch of a chosen river tributary, creek, or urban canal
(estero).

To maintain focus and prevent teams from overextending their
resources, proposals must allocate at least 70% of the Php 10
Million budget toward ONE main focus stream. The remaining
30% may be used for a supporting secondary activity. Every
proposal can be (or a combination of) Protocal-based, Service-
based, or Product-based Approach.
Teams must select their primary focus from the following three
options:
Stream 1: Household Trash Control & Local Waste
This option focuses on stopping garbage and dirty household
water before it enters the river. Physical solutions (i.e. river
trash traps, neighborhood drain filters, or small-scale septic
tank cleaning systems) may be paired with local government
ordinances. Policy actions may include municipal garbage-
sorting laws, local trash dumping fines, and household waste
management incentives.
Stream 2: Commercial & Industrial Pollution
This option focuses on tracking and stopping illegal waste
dumping from factories, markets, and commercial businesses.
Physical technologies (i.e. digital water quality monitors,
automated drain shut-off valves, or localized water filters) may
be paired with municipal enforcement rules. Policy actions may
include strict business discharge permits, mandatory public
=== PAGE 9 ===
problem statement
6
compliance reporting, and shared cleanup agreements among
local commercial establishments.
Stream 3: On-Site River Restoration & Environmental Zoning
This option focuses on cleaning and restoring the natural health
of the waterbody itself. Natural engineering solutions (i.e.
artificial floating plant islands, vegetated drainage channels, or
water-bubbling aeration systems) may be paired with land-use
policies. Some policy actions may include enforcing illegal
riverbank building bans (easement rules), protecting waterfront
green zones, and establishing community-led river stewardship
programs.
Important Note: Proposals attempting to solve every environmental
problem simultaneously or pitch multi-billion-peso infrastructure
overhauls will be penalized for lack of practical feasibility. The Php
10 Million budget and 12-month timeline are strictly designated to
prove that a single, focused solution works within a bounded 1-
kilometer pilot zone. Teams must demonstrate technical discipline,
budget realism, and clear policy planning at this local scale before
explaining how their solution scales up to major national rivers.
III. Geographic Prioritization
Participating teams are granted flexibility in selecting their
localized pilot site, while adhering to a selection of water bodies
for their project’s national expansion blueprint.

estero, coastal creek, municipal drainage canal, or river

realistic mapping of point/non-point pollution sources, and
accurate budget itemization under the allotted seed cap.

their solution can be adapted to address one of the top three most
plastic-polluted rivers in the Philippines. According to landmark

the task
In this preliminary phase of the Case Study Competition, each
team will be asked to submit their own concept project
proposals for an approach to address key causes of water
pollution in the Philippines. Participants may utilize any (or a
combination) of the following approaches below: 
1. Protocol-based Approach: This approach focuses on the
application of established guidelines and procedures to develop
strategic measures that enhance the efficiency, resilience, and
management of the LGU’s waterway systems. It primarily
employs a qualitative framework to ensure systematic
evaluation and continuous improvement.
2. Service-based Approach: This approach centers on
empowering communities and stakeholders to actively
participate in strengthening and modernizing waterway
management systems. It emphasizes capacity-building,
collaboration, and service delivery to improve accessibility,
sustainability, and overall productivity.
  
3. Product-based Approach: This approach involves the design,
development, and innovation of tangible technologies or
equipment to optimize the performance and sustainability of
waterway infrastructure. It highlights technological
advancement as a means to enhance reliability, efficiency, and
long-term viability in the waste control sector.

People’s Choice Award Mechanics
The People’s Choice Award will be conducted through two
official platforms: online voting (RF Facebook Page) and Google
Forms submission. This dual system is designed to make voting
accessible to a wider audience while preventing duplicate or
unfair entries.
1. Online Voting Platform – The posters for the Research
Competition and the Youth Science Convention – Case Study
Competition will be uploaded on the official Research Fair
Facebook page. Supporters may cast their votes by liking the
