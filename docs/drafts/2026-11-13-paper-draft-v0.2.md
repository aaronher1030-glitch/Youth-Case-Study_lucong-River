## The Last Meter
### Contested barriers to household desludging in a one-kilometre estero reach
**Estero de Paco, Manila — Stream 1: Household Trash Control & Local Waste**

### 1. Background

Estero de Paco is a 2.7–3.0 km tidal tributary of the Pasig River. It drains Paco and Pandacan through Barangays 671–687 and enters the Pasig beside Paco Market. The Pasig is classified Class C under DENR Administrative Order 2016-08: biochemical oxygen demand of 7 mg/L or less, dissolved oxygen of 5 mg/L or more, suspended solids of 80 mg/L or less, and fecal coliform of 200 MPN/100 mL or less.

Measured performance sits far outside those limits. Six main-stem stations averaged BOD of 27.8 mg/L in 2018 and 24.2 mg/L in 2019, roughly four times the standard. Across 2020–2021 sampling, seven of eight stations met the oxygen floor on neap tides but only one of eight did on spring tides. Three Manila City sites sampled on 25 January 2025 returned fecal coliform between 2.4×10⁵ and 1.1×10⁶ MPN/100 mL — between 1,200 and 5,500 times the Class C guideline. A water quality index computed across 2010–2024 scored all six stations "Poor" in every year, and the authors' own forecast to 2030 reports only marginal change.

The reach has not lacked attention. It was dredged and cleared in 2009, reopened in 2012 with a linear park and three aerators at roughly ₱20 million, and hosted a US$438,000 Asian Development Bank pilot from 2012 to 2015 that installed a condominial sewer, solar pumps, an anaerobic baffled reactor and a wetland for 58 households in Barangay 672. That system removed 87% of BOD — but only about 20% of fecal coliform, leaving effluent some 250 times the Class C limit, and monitoring ceased after three months. In March 2025 the DENR cleared structures to reclaim the three-metre easement over 1.7 km of bank. In the first half of 2025 alone, 872,625 sacks were removed from Metro Manila waterways by 1,467 estero rangers.

The water did not improve. That is where this proposal begins.

### 2. Diagnosis: the load is sewage

The most recent apportionment of the Laguna de Bay–Pasig–Manila Bay watershed attributes 83.44% of biochemical oxygen demand to domestic sources, 11.17% to industry and 5.14% to agriculture. The authors are explicit that this is a unit-load model, described as "a first pass estimate," computed "without considering the natural purification in river systems." It is the best available figure and it is not a measurement.

Measured evidence points the same way. Microbial source tracking across 55 sink samples from December 2021 to May 2023 found sewage accounted for 93% of all *attributable* fecal contamination — while 57% of the community on average remained unassigned, rising to 62% at downstream Pasig stations.

Solid waste is not the oxygen problem. Polyethylene exerts essentially no biochemical oxygen demand. Its harm is real but different: hydraulic blockage, plastic transport, vector breeding. Metro Manila's drainage system floods from garbage blockages on 49 streets. The useful division is therefore discipline-specific — septage dominates oxygen demand and fecal loading; solid waste dominates physical and plastic impacts. A proposal that claims otherwise will be caught.

One figure should be retired rather than cited. The "60% domestic, 33% industrial, 7% solid waste" split appears in Gorme et al. (2010) with no citation, contradicts that paper's own tables — which give 44/45/11 for 1990 and about 10% for solid waste in 2003 — and has been reproduced uncritically ever since.

### 3. The contested barrier

Eighty-four percent of Filipinos depend on septic tanks. In Metro Manila, desludging is free at the point of use, recovered through an environmental charge on the water bill, with both concessionaires publishing barangay schedules. Roughly 40% of customers — 6.88 million of 17.21 million — decline it. Acceptance measured 56.96% in 2024 and 55.16% in 2025, against a 60% target.

Two explanations compete, and neither has been tested at household scale.

**The access hypothesis.** In an 800-household survey across Metro Manila, Rizal, Laguna and Pampanga in 2016, 70% of tanks sat under the house, requiring the floor to be broken; 71% of access paths fitted only a person or a motorbike; and 18% of toilets were more than 20 metres from a road where a truck could park. Twenty-two percent of tanks had no cemented bottom. A standard tanker, however, carries 100 metres of hose, which places most of that 18% within normal reach. The hypothesis is plausible and partly self-defeating.

**The awareness hypothesis.** The regulator's own framing is that roughly seven million Metro Manila residents do not know the service is free; the MWSS funds an awareness survey in its 2025 scorecard. In a Metro Manila survey, 9.3% of households paid private desludgers ₱1,300–7,000 rather than wait for the free service. Against this, Baliwag Water District — flat terrain, wide roads, no access constraint — had only 20.09% of tanks desludged.

Neither explanation survives alone. Baliwag undermines access. The hose reach undermines the access statistic. Yet households paying for a free service undermines pure awareness failure too. What is missing is not a theory but a record: **no barangay-level tank register, no visit-level service record and no household-scale manifest reconciliation exist anywhere in the Philippines.** Every actor is arguing from anecdote.

That absence is the opportunity, and it is why a protocol design rather than a device is the right strategy. The competition asks teams to prove that a single bounded solution works. The most defensible thing a one-kilometre pilot can prove is *which barrier actually binds* — because until that is known, every larger program in the country is spending against a guess.

### 4. Design: one service, two arms

Four hundred households across one contiguous kilometre receive the same verified desludging service. They are assigned by block to one of two notification arms, because the barriers imply different interventions:

- **Arm 1 — standard notice.** The current practice: a schedule card through barangay channels.
- **Arm 2 — access-supported notice.** The same notice, plus access remediation where needed — floor works or an external access port — and a household visit that explains the free entitlement.

The primary endpoint is **tanks desludged within the scheduled window**. If access binds, Arm 2 should outperform Arm 1 materially. If awareness binds, both arms should perform similarly and both should exceed the 55% baseline once explanation is added. If neither moves, the barrier is something else — trust, landlord–tenant split, or fleet reliability — and the paper will say so.

Supporting components: a catchment register, a manifest chain to a permitted facility, and overflow alarms.

### 5. Why an alarm and not a sludge sensor

A septic tank's liquid level is fixed by its outlet invert and remains constant while sludge accumulates at 25–77 litres per person per year. Ultrasonic and pressure devices therefore measure the wrong variable; the Sanergy pilot in Nairobi abandoned pressure transducers for exactly this reason, and the only instruments that resolve a sludge blanket are lowered probes costing USD 1,000–15,000. A float-type high-level alarm costs ₱500–4,000, detects the event that actually discharges raw sewage, and fails safe. It is the only sensor role claimed.

### 6. Twelve-month plan

Months 1–2 establish permissions, the register, the access survey and eight weeks of bank observation. Month 3 commissions equipment, trains the crew and tests the manifest chain with a traceable load. Months 4–12 run nine collection months with monthly reconciliation. Month 12 evaluates and decides handover.

**Economics, stated without decoration.** Recurring cost is roughly ₱7,000 per household per year at 400 households, against a published benchmark of US$10–30 and an environmental-charge yield near ₱500–1,000. The pilot does not close that gap. Quantifying it is one of the results. Four lines carry no published Philippine benchmark — access remediation, the small vacuum unit, the register system and the gate fee — and are labelled assumptions, not prices.

### 8. Baselines and targets

| Measure | Baseline | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Tanks desludged within window | ~55% acceptance citywide | ≥80% Arm 2; report both arms |
| Arm 2 minus Arm 1 difference | Not measured | Report with 95% interval |
| Volume with matched manifest and receipt | Not measured | 100% |
| Overflow alarms dispositioned within 72 h | Not measured | 100% |
| Cost per household served | ₱25,000 year one | Report recurring cost separately |
| Fecal coliform and BOD at outfall | 2.4×10⁵–1.1×10⁶; 24.2–27.8 mg/L | Report concentration change |

No target is set for river load. A 1.58 m³ tank emptied once a decade releases about 0.43 litres a day, while four occupants discharge roughly 40 grams of BOD daily as effluent regardless. Full compliance abates a small fraction of one tank's flux. Claiming otherwise would be arithmetic theatre.

### 9. National scaling blueprint

The scaling argument follows a funding gap. The DPWH sewerage and septage programme was designed for LGUs outside Metro Manila, and by 2025 only two LGUs nationwide held subsidy approval. The five septage plants serving the metropolis are not the constraint: Manila Water operated at 58% of capacity and Maynilad at 35% in 2024. The constraint is the last household.

Stage one replicates the register and two-arm protocol across two or three Pasig-connected esteros. Stage two extends to Tullahan, where the same access constraint plausibly governs. Stage three addresses Meycauayan only after industrial co-contamination is screened, because a household program cannot answer a metals problem.

### 10. Implications

**Environment.** No structure enters the channel, no drain is closed, no sediment is disturbed. The intervention removes oxygen-demanding and fecal loading at source. It claims no restoration: the 83% domestic share is a model, 57% of measured fecal sources are unassigned, and one reach is not a river.

**Health.** Thirty-six percent of under-five children in low-income Metro Manila reported diarrhoea in the preceding four weeks. The pilot claims reduced exposure opportunity, not reduced disease. No student or volunteer touches septage; occupational safety law reaches employees only, so that rule is project policy, not a legal citation.

**Community.** The barangay holds the register, sets the collection window and receives the manifest record. Handover requires a named budget holder before month 12, or the equipment is removed and the register transferred.

### 11. Limitations

The 2016 survey is a four-province comparator, not a Paco measurement. No measured apportionment of BOD exists for the Pasig. No Philippine study isolates septage as a health exposure. One reach provides no spatial replication, and the two arms are block-assigned rather than randomised. Concurrent national programs — Kalinisan sa Bagong Pilipinas, Bayanihan sa Estero, weekly barangay cleanups — will confound any observed change, and will be reported rather than adjusted away. The measured water-quality trend has been flat for fifteen years, and a one-kilometre, twelve-month pilot should not be expected to bend it.
