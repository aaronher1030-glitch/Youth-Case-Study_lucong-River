## 2. Introduction to the Proposed Approach

The Lucong River (Lucung in the municipal record) drains Concepcion, Tarlac and its rice and sugarcane fields before it joins the Chico River (Municipal Government of Concepcion, 2024). The study reach runs 1.0 km downstream from Santiago Bridge (15.3300° N, 120.6284° E), the site of an EMB sampling station, to 15.3334° N, 120.6367° E. The full bridge-to-bridge reach measures 1.52 km (Figure 1). Both banks lie in Barangays Santiago and Alfonso, two of the municipality's seven urban barangays, with 15,220 residents and 3,148 households between them (PhilAtlas, 2026).

The reach meets the competition's site selection conditions. DENR's River for Life program listed the Lucong among Tarlac's pollution-vulnerable water bodies (Punto! Central Luzon, 2019). Fecal coliform at the EMB station reached 16,000 MPN/100 mL in the October 2024 to 2025 round, 80 times the 200 MPN/100 mL standard, while BOD (5 mg/L), DO (6.7 mg/L), pH (7.83) and temperature (30.4 °C) stayed within DAO 2016-08 limits (Table 1). The bridge gauge read 3.7 m on 22 July 2025 (MDRRMO Concepcion Tarlac, 2025).

The pressures come from how the banks are used. Housing and roadside shops crowd both bridge approaches, and paddies and farm lots sit behind the levees. Neither barangay has a sewer network, so households depend on septic tanks. Collection trucks run fixed routes, yet plastic and food packaging still gather below Santiago Bridge (team photographs, November 2025). Road drains and commercial lots empty into the channel. The regional pattern is commercial, not backyard: Bamban, on the southwest boundary, lists 21 livestock and poultry establishments in its land use plan, 14 of them piggeries, four in Anupul, the barangay nearest the head of this channel (Municipality of Bamban, 2026). Whether their runoff reaches the reach is untested.

EMB attributes the coliform exceedance to domestic wastewater and agricultural runoff, and reports livestock farms discharging sewage into the Lucong (EMB Region III, 2024). The chemistry agrees. BOD and DO within limits mean the load lacks the oxygen demand of industrial effluent; the signal is fecal. The team therefore reads unmonitored commercial runoff, mainly piggeries, as the main driver, with inadequate sewerage close behind and uncollected solid waste adding to the load. The reach has no heavy industry.

Month 1 will test that reading. If fecal coliform does not rise below the piggery clusters, the diagnosis shifts to household sewerage; three 100 square metre bank quadrats will measure the visible waste; and MENRO's outfall inventory and inspection records will replace the placeholders this proposal flags.

Concepcion has tried the rule. In 2017 the Sangguniang Bayan ordered a 10,000-head piggery 500 m from the town centre closed; the provincial board voided the order, and the zoning ordinance behind it had never been approved (Tarlac Weekender, 2017). The rule failed and the manure stayed.

## 3. Suggested Process or Product

**The product is a service, not a device.** The team proposes a shed-level manure and washwater service: every piggery near the reach gets a sealed sump, a weekly collection, on-site digestion, and a compost product that returns to the rice farms behind the levees. All of the spending sits in Stream 2, commercial and industrial pollution, where the competition expects enforcement paired with physical works. This entry supplies both: the manifest is the compliance reporting, and the permit condition is the enforcement.

A single treatment plant at the bridge was considered and rejected. It would need a sewer network that does not exist and cannot be built inside ₱10 million. Collecting at the shed needs no pipe in the ground, treats the load before it dilutes, and produces something the farms will take.

**Plan of action.** The service runs 24 months in four phases.

*Months 1 to 3, verify.* Census the sheds with MENRO and the barangay business registry, GPS every drain mouth, and take baseline samples above and below each cluster. Secure a written land consent for the treatment site and agree the permit condition with the Mayor's office.

*Months 4 to 8, build.* Construct three covered digesters, the composting pad and the hardstanding. Procure the tanker and carts. Train two operators and the barangay monitors.

*Months 9 to 18, operate.* Weekly collection on a fixed route, one manifest per load, quarterly sampling, and compost turned every ten days.

*Months 19 to 24, verify and transfer.* Run the audit, publish the cost curve, and hand the service to a barangay cooperative or to MENRO.

**Raw materials.** The inputs are the waste itself: about 450 kg of pig manure and 5 cubic metres of washwater a day. Rice husk from local mills supplies the carbon for composting at roughly three parts husk to one part digestate. Water dilutes the slurry at start-up, and finished digestate seeds the next module.

**Steps.**

1. Each shed gets a concrete sump and a 200-litre drum, so manure and washwater never reach the bank.
2. A 2,000-litre vacuum tanker runs a fixed weekly route; every load is weighed and logged.
3. Slurry enters a 25-cubic-metre covered digester and stays 10 to 15 days. Gas collects under an HDPE cover.
4. The gas, about 30 cubic metres a day or 18 kg of LPG equivalent, heats the composting pad's water and fires the sheds' scalding tanks.
5. Digestate is dewatered, mixed with rice husk, and windrowed under a roof for 45 days, turned every ten.
6. Compost goes to the rice farms behind the levees at no charge in year one.

**Efficiency.** The site receives 450 kg of manure and about 5 cubic metres of washwater a day and ships out compost, not slurry. Digestion converts roughly a third of the volatile solids to gas and strips the material of its odour and most of its pathogen load; the thermophilic composting phase finishes the job. Nothing is discharged to the river, and the nutrient that leaves the farms returns to them.

## 4. Discussion of the Expected End Output and Its Functions

The end output is an audited shed-level waste service: the physical works, a manifest for every load from shed to digester to field, and an unbroken monitoring series at the reach.

It does five things. It removes the load the diagnosis identified, which is the whole point of the entry. It produces compliance evidence, because a manifest turns an invisible discharge into a traceable transaction and gives the LGU something to inspect against. It creates a cost curve that a second municipality can copy without repeating the research. It converts an informal activity into a priced, permitted service, so shed owners gain security instead of risk. And it returns value locally: biogas to the sheds, compost to the farms.

The record matters as much as the concrete. Concepcion's Citizen's Charter already requires a barangay resolution and health certificates before a piggery may obtain a Mayor's permit (Municipal Government of Concepcion, 2024). The service attaches to that existing gate, so no new ordinance is needed for the pilot.

The manifest is one sheet per load: shed, date, weight, driver, digester bay, and the field that takes the compost. A barangay monitor countersigns it. Twelve months of manifests would give the reach its first discharge record.

The novelty is not the digester; covered lagoons have run on Philippine farms for decades. It is the coupling: a shed keeps its Mayor's permit only if every load is collected and manifested, so compliance and service become one transaction instead of two separate inspections.

## 5. Feasibility and Economic Analysis

**Overall plan.** Facilities are three digesters, a roofed composting pad, hardstanding, and a records office at the treatment site. Supply is secured by the service contract itself: sheds must be connected to keep their permit. No compost revenue is counted, so the budget does not depend on a market that has not been tested.

At the documented 15-shed cluster the public cost is about ₱25,600 per shed-month. That number falls as fixed costs spread: about ₱6,400 at 60 sheds. If the census finds 30 sheds, three more modules and a second tanker add ₱2.6 million, which the contingency, revolving fund and replication lines cover only after trimming. A larger census changes the shape of the budget, not the design.

**What will be measured.** Four indicators, reported quarterly. Manifest coverage, the share of shed-weeks collected, targeted at 95 per cent. Fecal coliform at Santiago Bridge, from 16,000 down to 2,000 MPN/100 mL by Month 24, an 88 per cent cut that is still ten times the standard; 200 follows by Month 36 if the piggery share holds. Manure diverted, 164 tonnes a year at the documented cluster. And cost per shed-month, tracked so the curve is measured rather than projected. All four get published whether or not they flatter the service.

**Technical feasibility.** Covered lagoon and fixed-dome digesters are standard Philippine farm equipment, documented locally in the PCARRD-funded Vigan swine digester (Tacbas et al., 2010). Tankers, pumps and turners are off-the-shelf and locally fabricated. No imported proprietary process is involved, and the operators need trade skills, not a laboratory.

**National scaling blueprint.** The same EMB table that condemns the Lucong lists five more failing Tarlac stations: Bintauan Creek, Masalasa Creek, Rio Chico, Cutcut River and the Bamban River. Bamban, the municipality on Concepcion's southwest boundary, has already ratified a numeric siting schedule: 25 m from groundwater, 500 m from a highway for commercial piggeries, 1,000 m from built-up areas (Municipality of Bamban, 2026). Tarlac therefore has a diagnosed set of reaches and a regulatory template. What it lacks is the service. The blueprint is one reach, one manifest, one cost curve, then copy.

## 6. Implications

**Environment.** The service removes the manure load before it reaches the Chico and the wider Pampanga system, and the monitoring series makes the gain auditable. It also creates three hazards of its own: methane escaping an imperfect cover, digestate spread past what the fields can absorb, and slurry spilled in transit. Each has a countermeasure built into the design. Covers are gas-tight and pressure-tested quarterly, and surplus gas is flared rather than vented. Compost is applied to a nutrient budget agreed with the Municipal Agriculture Office, not by eye. The tanker carries drip trays and washes down at a bay that drains back into the digester.

**Health and safety.** The workers carry the real risk: hydrogen sulphide in the digester headspace, drums lifted by hand, wet concrete underfoot. Entry follows a confined-space protocol with a gas meter, a harness and a second person at the hatch. Operators get boots, gloves and vaccination for tetanus and leptospirosis. For the surrounding community the direction reverses. The bank stops receiving raw manure, so exposure at the washing and bathing points falls, and the smell with it.

**Community involvement.** Shed owners get biogas, compost and a permit they can defend. Barangay monitors walk the route and sign the manifests. Farmers receive the compost. The service is run by people who live on the reach, which is the only version of it that survives the grant.
