## The Last Meter
### A statutory desludging mandate delivered at a third of its required rate
**Estero de Paco, Manila — Stream 1: Household Trash Control & Local Waste**

### 1. Background

Estero de Paco is a 2.7–3.0 km tidal tributary of the Pasig, draining Paco and Pandacan through Barangays 671–687 and entering the Pasig beside Paco Market. The Pasig is Class C under DENR Administrative Order 2016-08: biochemical oxygen demand of 7 mg/L or less, dissolved oxygen of 5 mg/L or more, suspended solids of 80 mg/L or less, and fecal coliform of 200 MPN/100 mL or less.

Measured performance sits far outside those limits. Six main-stem stations averaged BOD of 27.8 mg/L in 2018 and 24.2 mg/L in 2019, roughly four times the standard. Three Manila City sites sampled on 25 January 2025 returned fecal coliform between 2.4×10⁵ and 1.1×10⁶ MPN/100 mL — between 1,200 and 5,500 times the guideline. A water quality index computed across 2010–2024 scored all six stations "Poor" in every year.

The reach has not lacked attention: dredged in 2009, reopened in 2012 with a linear park and three aerators at roughly ₱20 million, and host to a US$438,000 ADB pilot from 2012 to 2015 whose condominial sewer, baffled reactor and wetland served 58 households in Barangay 672 — removing 87% of BOD but only 20% of fecal coliform, with monitoring stopped after three months. In March 2025 the DENR cleared the three-metre easement over 1.7 km of bank.

The water did not improve. Fifteen years of intervention produced a flat trend. This proposal does not ask why the estero is dirty. It asks **why a service that is already mandated, already paid for through a charge on every water bill, and already scheduled by barangay, fails at a household's doorstep.**

### 2. Diagnosis: the load is sewage

The most recent apportionment of the Laguna de Bay–Pasig–Manila Bay watershed attributes 83.44% of biochemical oxygen demand to domestic sources, 11.17% to industry and 5.14% to agriculture — an explicit unit-load model, "a first pass estimate," computed "without considering the natural purification in river systems." The best available figure, and not a measurement.

Measured evidence agrees: microbial source tracking across 55 sink samples from December 2021 to May 2023 found sewage accounted for 93% of attributable fecal contamination, while 57% of the community remained unassigned, rising to 62% downstream.

Solid waste is not the oxygen problem. Polyethylene exerts essentially no biochemical oxygen demand. Its harm is real but different: hydraulic blockage, plastic transport, vector breeding — Metro Manila's drainage floods from garbage blockages on 49 streets. Septage dominates oxygen demand and fecal loading; solid waste dominates physical and plastic impacts.

### 3. The gap: a duty that exists, and is not delivered

Manila has already legislated the remedy. Ordinance No. 8491 (2016) requires desludging every three to five years or at one-third sludge volume, requires an Environmental Sanitation Certificate at permit stage, and makes **free residential desludging every five years an obligation of the water concessionaires**, recovered through the environmental charge. Ordinance No. 8793 (2021) requires a certificate from a DENR-accredited transporter. The Revised Concession Agreement sets the interval at **five to seven years**. The charge has stood on every bill since the concession began, rising from 20% to 25% in January 2025. The city keeps a desludging database and accredits haulers.

Law, money, obligation, registry and permit condition all exist. Delivery does not.

Maynilad emptied **98,216 septic tanks in 2024** against roughly **1.53 million billed west-zone accounts**: about **6.4% a year**, an implied cycle near fifteen and a half years, against a statutory five to seven. The coverage figure is not a delivery figure — **801,221 accounts were *offered* desludging** across 2022–2026. Offered is not emptied, and no reconciliation is published.

Nor is there an indicator that would expose the gap. No agency publishes households desludged as standing statistics — not the Field Health Services Information System, which reports facility access, not the PSA, the DILG, or the MWSS Regulatory Office. The regulator sets no septage-specific target and imposes no septage-specific penalty; its ₱54.28 million in bill rebates in April 2026 concerned supply interruptions.

The failure therefore sits inside a system fully equipped on paper. It needs no new law, and this pilot proposes none: it is a delivery failure between the schedule and the tank — the last metre. The decision that could fund a remedy has a date: Manila's FY2028 Annual Investment Plan must be approved by **7 June 2027**, with new lines inserted by **April 2027**; as a highly urbanised city, Manila's budget receives no national review.

### 4. The contested barrier

Eighty-four percent of Filipinos depend on septic tanks. In Metro Manila desludging is free at point of use and both concessionaires publish barangay schedules — yet roughly 40% of customers, 6.88 million of 17.21 million, decline it. Acceptance was 56.96% in 2024 and 55.16% in 2025 against a 60% target.

Two explanations compete; neither is tested at household scale.

**The access hypothesis.** An 800-household survey in 2016 across Metro Manila, Rizal, Laguna and Pampanga found 70% of tanks under the house requiring floor work, 71% of access paths fitting only a person or motorbike, and 18% of toilets more than 20 metres from a road. A standard tanker carries 100 metres of hose, placing most of that 18% within reach — plausible, and partly self-defeating.

**The awareness hypothesis.** Roughly seven million Metro Manila residents are said not to know the service is free, and 9.3% paid private desludgers ₱1,300–7,000 rather than wait. Baliwag Water District — flat terrain, wide roads — desludged only 20.09%.

Neither survives alone. What is missing is not a theory but a record: **no barangay-level tank register, no visit-level service record and no household-scale manifest reconciliation exist anywhere in the Philippines.** The pilot's job is to produce the first, because until the barrier is identified every larger programme spends against a guess.

### 5. Design: one service, two arms

Four hundred households across one contiguous kilometre receive the same verified service, assigned by block to one of two arms.

- **Arm 1 — standard notice.** The current practice: a schedule card through barangay channels.
- **Arm 2 — access-supported notice.** The same notice, plus access remediation where needed — floor works or an external access port — and a household visit explaining the free entitlement.

The primary endpoint is tanks desludged within the scheduled window. If access binds, Arm 2 should outperform materially. If awareness binds, both arms should perform similarly and exceed the 55% baseline once explanation is added. If neither moves, the barrier is trust, tenure or fleet reliability, and the paper will say so.

Supporting components: a catchment register, a manifest chain to a permitted facility, and overflow alarms. A queue simulation of the 400 households returns the arm difference as a distribution: at zero awareness effect Arm 2 leads by 13.7 points, of which 8.5 is access work and 5.2 response speed.

### 6. Why an alarm and not a sludge sensor

A tank's liquid level is fixed by its outlet invert and stays constant while sludge accumulates at 25–77 litres per person per year. Ultrasonic and pressure devices measure the wrong variable; the Sanergy pilot in Nairobi abandoned pressure transducers for this reason, and only lowered probes costing USD 1,000–15,000 resolve a sludge blanket. A float-type high-level alarm costs ₱500–4,000, detects the event that discharges raw sewage, and fails safe.

### 7. Twelve-month plan

Months 1–2 establish permissions, the register, the access survey and eight weeks of bank observation. Month 3 commissions equipment, trains the crew and tests the manifest chain. Months 4–12 run nine collection months with monthly reconciliation; month 12 evaluates and decides handover.

### 9. Baselines and targets

| Measure | Baseline | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Tanks desludged within window | Measured in Month 2; not assumed | ≥80% Arm 2; report both arms |
| Arm 2 minus Arm 1 difference | Not measured anywhere | Report with 95% interval |
| Volume with matched manifest and receipt | Not measured | 100% |
| Access tier recorded | 0% | 100% of reach |
| Cost per household served | ₱25,000 year one | Report recurring cost separately |

No target is set for river load. A 1.58 m³ tank emptied once a decade releases about 0.43 litres a day, while four occupants discharge roughly 40 grams of BOD daily regardless — full compliance abates a small fraction of one tank's flux. Claiming otherwise would be arithmetic theatre.

### 10. National scaling: a readiness rule

Most scaling arguments state an intention; this one is a test another city can apply without us.

A city is ready when it has **(a)** a desludging mandate on the books, **(b)** a concessionaire under a five-to-seven-year obligation, **(c)** a published tank registry, and **(d)** an unspent development fund. Where all four hold, the intervention is **enforcement** — the pilot supplies access and protocol, not money for service. Where (a) and (b) hold but (d) fails, it is **procurement**, and the argument shifts to tariff and subsidy. Where (c) fails, the first year is **registry-building**.

The **Pasig** — including Estero de Pandacan, which the brief itself nominates — is an enforcement case: Manila's ordinance and Maynilad's obligation both hold. The **Tullahan** depends on the LGU ordinance. **Meycauayan** is excluded for now: a household programme cannot answer an industrial metals problem, and saying so is what makes the other two credible.

### 11. Implications

**Environment.** No structure enters the channel, no drain is closed, no sediment disturbed. The intervention removes oxygen-demanding and fecal loading at source, and claims no restoration: the 83% domestic share is a model, 57% of measured fecal sources are unassigned, and one reach is not a river.

**Health.** Thirty-six percent of under-five children in low-income Metro Manila reported diarrhoea in the preceding four weeks. The pilot claims reduced exposure opportunity, not reduced disease. No student or volunteer touches septage; occupational safety law reaches employees only, so that rule is project policy, not a legal citation.

**Community.** The barangay holds the register, sets the window and receives the manifest record; the city receives a formatted indicator definition it does not currently have. Handover requires a named budget holder before month 12, or the equipment is removed and the register transferred.

### 12. Limitations

The 2016 survey is a four-province comparator, not a Paco measurement. No measured BOD apportionment exists for the Pasig, and no Philippine study isolates septage as a health exposure. One reach gives no spatial replication, and the arms are block-assigned rather than randomised. **Paco is reported among Manila's sewered districts at 35% citywide sewerage coverage; if most reach households are in fact connected, the septage framing is wrong for this site and the reach must move.** Concurrent programmes — Kalinisan sa Bagong Pilipinas, Bayanihan sa Estero, weekly cleanups — will confound observed change and will be reported, not adjusted away. The trend has been flat for fifteen years; a one-kilometre, twelve-month pilot should not be expected to bend it.
